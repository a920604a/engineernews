Here's something that might sound familiar. You're in your thirties or forties, you have more resources, more experience, more connections than you ever did at twenty — and somehow, you feel *more* stuck.

That's not a paradox. It's actually a pattern, and a Chinese criminal law professor named Luo Xiang put his finger on exactly why it happens.

His argument: the feeling of being trapped rarely comes from having fewer options. It comes from becoming less willing to actually choose between them.

Think about how kids make decisions. They want something, they say so. They don't like something, they say so. There's very little "but what if this leads to that, which leads to the other thing." The imagination of consequences just isn't that developed yet.

Now think about yourself today. You can trace the chain — if I do A, probably B happens, which risks C, which could mean D. That's not a flaw. That ability is genuinely useful. But it also makes every choice feel heavier, more expensive, more loaded.

And here's where loss aversion kicks in. Psychologically, losing something hurts roughly twice as much as gaining the same thing feels good. So when you're standing at a decision point, "not choosing" starts to feel like protection. Like you're keeping all your options alive.

But that's the trap hiding inside the trap. Not choosing doesn't freeze the situation — it just means external forces make the choice for you. Your career drifts. Your relationships drift. The direction of your life gets shaped by inertia and circumstance rather than by anything you actually decided. Delay isn't neutrality. It's just outsourcing the decision.

So what does it mean to actually have the courage to choose? And here Luo Xiang makes a distinction worth sitting with: he's not talking about recklessness. He's not describing the person who doesn't care about outcomes and just barrels forward. That's closer to numbness than courage.

Real courage in choosing looks like this: you see the cost clearly, you feel the weight of it — and you choose anyway, and you own what follows. That's not the carefree boldness of youth. It's something more like maturity.

A few things seem to help in practice.

First — be honest about the difference between "I can't" and "I'm afraid to." Most of the time, when we say we can't do something, what we actually mean is that we're uncertain about the outcome and we don't want to find out. That's worth naming accurately. Not to force yourself to be braver, just to be more honest.

Second — accept that most real choices are made with incomplete information. Waiting for the perfect moment, the perfect option, the perfect confidence — that's usually just procrastination wearing a more respectable coat. You choose, then you adjust based on what happens.

And third — make friends with the idea of regret. A lot of inaction is really just fear of future regret. But regret is survivable. It's not a life sentence. It's information.

Here's the summary. Feeling trapped is a signal — and what it's usually signaling is not that you have no options, but that you've been avoiding the act of choosing itself. Growing up doesn't make choices easier. The stakes feel higher because they often are. But knowing that the weight you're feeling isn't the absence of options — it's the presence of real consequences you can see clearly — that reframe matters. You're not stuck. You're hesitating. And those two things call for very different responses.
