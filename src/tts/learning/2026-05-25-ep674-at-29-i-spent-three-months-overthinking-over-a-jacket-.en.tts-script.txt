Three months. Ninety-something days. That's how long I spent going back and forth over whether to buy a jacket.

Not a luxury item. Not some agonizing financial stretch. Just an ordinary jacket, well within budget. And yet — I'd open the page, read reviews, close the tab. Open it again. Ask a friend. Compare two brands. Imagine outfits. Circle back to the reviews. Repeat.

Here's the thing I only figured out much later: I wasn't thinking about a jacket at all.

At 29, I was sitting on a pile of questions I couldn't face directly. Change jobs? Leave the city? Go back to school? These were real, heavy, uncomfortable questions with no clean answers. Too big to grab onto. Too scary to sit with.

So my brain found a workaround. It handed me a jacket and said — here, think about this instead. I could feel productive, deliberate, like I was taking something seriously — without ever having to look at the things that actually frightened me. The jacket became what you might call a proxy problem. A safe container for anxiety that had nowhere else to go.

This is a surprisingly common pattern. Overthinking almost never lives where it appears to live.

Now, you've probably heard the standard advice: just give yourself a deadline. When the timer runs out, commit. And look, it sounds sensible. But it tends to fail because it misdiagnoses the problem. It treats overthinking as a time management issue when it's really an emotional one. If anxiety is the root cause, a deadline doesn't dissolve the anxiety — it just compresses it. You end up anxious faster, not clearer.

What actually cuts through? One question. When you notice yourself looping on a decision for longer than makes any rational sense, ask yourself: *if I stopped thinking about this entirely right now, what would I have to face instead?*

That question tends to land somewhere real, fast.

For me, the answer was immediate: if I stopped obsessing over the jacket, I'd have to sit with the fact that I didn't know what I was doing with my career. And that was genuinely uncomfortable.

So how did it end? I bought the jacket. The actual purchase took maybe five minutes. I wear it sometimes. It's fine.

But here's what's interesting — I did eventually change jobs. That was the decision that had actually been keeping me up at night. And strangely, that one took me far less time than the jacket did. Because by then, I understood what was actually happening. The real problem was the real problem. I didn't need a stand-in anymore.

So three things to take with you.

First: overthinking is a signal, not a habit to optimize. It's your mind telling you there's something you're not ready to look at directly. Don't just try to speed through it — get curious about what it's pointing at.

Second: the question "how do I decide faster?" is often the wrong question. The better one is "what am I avoiding behind this decision?"

And third: once you identify the real source of the anxiety, the proxy decision tends to resolve itself almost instantly. The looping stops. The fog clears. Because you were never actually stuck on that decision to begin with.
