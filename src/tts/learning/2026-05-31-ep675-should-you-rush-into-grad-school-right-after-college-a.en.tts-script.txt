So near the end of college, this question shows up almost like a rite of passage: "Should I go to grad school?" And here's the funny thing — most people don't actually arrive at that question through careful thinking. They start thinking about it because someone else asked them. A relative at dinner, a friend filling out applications, a professor. Suddenly it's on your plate.

This episode of Adult's Small Talk digs into exactly that: is there a framework that's actually useful for deciding whether to go straight to grad school right after undergrad?

Let me give you the punchline up front, because it cuts through a lot of noise. There is no universal answer to "should I go straight to grad school." Anyone who tells you there is, is selling you something. The far more useful question is this: do you have a specific problem that grad school would actually help you solve? If yes — go. If not — go out and accumulate that problem first, then come back and ask again.

So let's start where you should actually start, which is not "should I go" but "why do I want to go." And you have to be honest with yourself here.

There are good reasons — and by good, I mean grad school as a targeted solution to something. Like, you want to enter a field that genuinely requires a graduate degree just to get in the door — think certain academic research roles, some medical-adjacent fields. Or you've hit a specific knowledge gap in your work, something concrete that graduate training would directly address. Or you've got a clear research direction and you want a focused academic environment to develop it. In all of these, the degree is a tool pointed at a target.

Then there's the other category — the reasons worth examining a little more skeptically. "Because I don't know what else to do." "Because everyone around me is going." "Because a graduate degree helps with job applications." Now, that last one is genuinely true in some fields — but notice you have to ask, which fields, specifically? For which roles?

Here's the thing: being in that second category doesn't automatically disqualify you. But if that's your primary motivation — if it's really just "I don't know what's next" — then grad school might simply postpone your uncertainty by two years, and hand you some debt on the way out. You haven't solved the problem. You've deferred it.

Now let's talk about the real fork in the road: working first versus going directly.

If you work first and then go back — the big advantage is that you know why you're there. The coursework connects to real situations. All those practical confusions you ran into on the job? They suddenly click into place in the classroom. And it runs both ways — abstract academic theory is so much easier to absorb and hold onto when you've already got the working vocabulary to hang it on. The downside, honestly, is psychological. You have to re-adjust to being a student again, and if you've gotten comfortable with a paycheck, going back to a low or no income takes some real recalibration.

Going straight through after undergrad — the appeal is lower time cost. You stay on the student track, you don't give up income you've already started earning because you never started earning it. And for people who are genuinely certain about the academic path, or for whom that degree is truly a barrier to entry — this is completely reasonable. No hesitation. The catch, though, is that a lot of people go directly out of inertia, not clarity. And a degree earned without a clear purpose has a way of showing up later. Picture yourself in an interview, and someone asks, "So what did grad school give you?" — and you can't quite articulate it. That's the risk.

Alright, let me leave you with a few practical questions actually worth sitting down and answering.

First: do the roles you're actually targeting require a master's? In a lot of engineering and product jobs, a graduate degree is a plus — not a requirement. So go research the real prerequisites for the positions you want before you commit years to it.

Second: will this specific program connect you to the network you want to be in? Because the value of grad school was never only the credential. It's the faculty connections, the industry ties, the alumni network. Honestly, those factors matter as much as rankings when you're choosing where to go.

And third — this is the self-test I like most — can you clearly describe what you'll do after you graduate? If you can't answer that cleanly, it doesn't mean you shouldn't go. But it does mean there's still some thinking left to do before you sign up.

So let me pull the threads together. First takeaway: grad school isn't inherently right or wrong, and there's no correct timing — stop looking for the standard answer, because there isn't one. Second: the question that actually cuts through everything is "am I going to solve a specific problem, or am I going because I haven't figured out what's next?" And third: if the answer is that you haven't figured it out — that's fine, but the move is to go accumulate that problem in the real world first, then revisit this. A degree pointed at a real target tends to lead to a choice you won't regret. A degree used to hide from uncertainty usually just delays the reckoning.
