Have you ever been in the middle of a perfectly normal day — maybe eating lunch, maybe staring at a task you need to start — and suddenly this thought hits you: everyone dies in a few decades anyway, so what does any of this actually matter?

And just like that, everything in front of you goes flat.

If that's happened to you, I want to talk about why — because it's not a sign that you've somehow seen through the illusion of life and found the cold truth underneath. It's actually a calibration problem. A very specific one.

Here's what's really going on. Every person carries an implicit time frame in their head — a default lens through which they evaluate things. Some people's default is "today." Does this meal taste good right now, is this problem solved right now. Others think in terms of years — how does this choice affect my career. And then there are people whose default lens is decades, or even centuries. The direction of civilization. The meaning of individual existence.

That last mode isn't a flaw. It's actually a real cognitive capacity. But here's where it breaks: if you're using a cosmic, decades-long lens to evaluate *every single daily action*, almost everything will fail the test. Not because those things lack value — but because you're using the wrong measuring stick.

Think about meaning for a second. Meaning is deeply tied to time scale. From a truly cosmic perspective, nothing is permanent — that's technically true. But meaning doesn't need to be permanent to be real. A conversation with a friend that actually went somewhere. An afternoon so absorbing you forgot to check your phone. Those things have meaning. They just evaporate the second you ask "will this matter in five hundred years?" The conversation didn't fail you. The question did.

This mismatch shows up in a few recognizable patterns. One is what I'd call pre-action paralysis — asking "will this matter in five years?" before you start anything. Sounds like long-term thinking. It's actually procrastination wearing strategic clothing. Another pattern is a kind of chronic half-presence — you're always mentally preparing for some future version of your life, which means the actual life happening right now never quite lands. And then there's the subtlest one: using the "nothing matters" frame as emotional armor. If you don't let anything feel meaningful, you can't be hurt when you lose it. That's not philosophy — that's protection.

So how do you recalibrate without losing the long view entirely?

The key word is *switch* — not abandon. Long-horizon thinking is genuinely useful for big decisions: should I change careers, should I relocate, where am I trying to go. It's not useful for deciding whether *this specific afternoon* was worth having. Those are different questions. They need different lenses.

Medium-term anchors help a lot. "Where do I want to be in three months?" generates real traction — it's close enough to feel actionable, far enough to see past today's noise. And perhaps most importantly: let meaning be temporary. This project might not matter in three years. But right now it's teaching you something, and it's giving you a sense of forward motion. That's sufficient. Temporary meaning is still meaning.

Here's where I'd leave you with three things to carry out of this:

First — the hollow feeling isn't a sign you've seen too clearly. It's a sign your time scale is miscalibrated. The world isn't emptier than you thought; your lens is just set to the wrong distance.

Second — long-term vision and present engagement aren't in conflict. The goal is to be able to move between distances — to zoom out when a big decision needs it, and to zoom all the way back in when what the moment needs is your actual attention.

And third — meaning doesn't need to last forever to count. The standard of "will this matter in five hundred years" would disqualify almost everything worth doing. Don't let it disqualify yours.
