You've got a book on your shelf — maybe more than one — that you bought with real enthusiasm, started, then quietly abandoned. And every time you walk past it, there's this low-grade guilt. Like you owe it something.

That guilt has a name. And Hao Xu-Lie, the Taiwanese financial author and host of the Haovoice podcast, has a pretty liberating take on it: not finishing is normal. And it's fine.

Here's his core argument. The purpose of reading is not turning all the pages. It's finding a connection between the book and your own life. That's it. Once that connection happens, the book has done its job.

And that connection can look like a lot of things. Maybe a concept suddenly clarifies something you'd always half-understood — like "oh, *that's* why I've always thought this way." Maybe a premise in the book makes you uncomfortable because it directly challenges an assumption you've been carrying around. Maybe it's a method you can use tomorrow, or a story that mirrors something in your own experience. Any of those counts. All of those count.

So where does the guilt come from? Probably school. In school, books go front to back, you're supposed to remember the content because there's a test, and "finishing" is the measurable standard. That framework made complete sense in school. But here's the thing — school books were designed to move everyone through the same knowledge at the same pace. The books you pick up at a bookstore were never designed for everyone. They're what *you*, at a specific point in your life, felt you needed.

What you needed might be on page 30. It might be scattered across three chapters. Page 180 might have absolutely nothing to do with your life right now, and grinding through it burns your attention without building any real understanding.

Now, "just find the connection" sounds easy — almost too easy. But Hao's not talking about shallow recognition, the "oh yeah I've heard of this" feeling. He means the moment where something in the book connects to something *real* in your situation, and you actually have to stop. You close the book. Something turns over in your mind. You start reconsidering a decision you made, or you finally see a new angle on a problem you've been sitting with for weeks.

That's actually *harder* to achieve than reading 300 pages with nothing sticking. The real measure of reading density isn't word count — it's connection density. Some books you finish in a weekend and a year later you remember nothing. Some books you read three chapters of, and one idea from those three chapters shapes how you think for years.

For anyone reading technical material — system design books, architecture guides, programming references — this framework is especially useful. You usually pick up a technical book because of a specific problem you're trying to solve. Reading front to back is almost never the right strategy. A better move: scan the table of contents, find the chapters that match where you are right now, read those, follow your questions to related sections, and then put the book somewhere accessible so you can return to it the next time your life needs it. Treat it as a knowledge repository, not a task to complete.

Hao also makes one more interesting point about the act of buying books in the first place. Even a book you never finish reflects your state and your curiosity at the moment you bought it. And that's a signal worth paying attention to. "Why did I think this book mattered to me then?" is a much more useful question than "Why didn't I finish it?" The first helps you understand yourself. The second just produces guilt.

So here are the things worth holding onto. One: the goal of reading is connection, not completion — once the book has given you something real, it's done its job. Two: the guilt comes from applying school rules to adult reading, and those rules were never designed for the books you choose yourself. And three: a book you didn't finish isn't a failure. It's a record of who you were when you picked it up — and it'll be there waiting when your life catches up to what's on page 180.
