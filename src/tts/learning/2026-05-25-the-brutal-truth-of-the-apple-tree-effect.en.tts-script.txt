There's a type of person who gets stuck making decisions — not because they don't know what they want, but because they want the *best* option. And that clarity, ironically, is exactly what freezes them.

It's called the Apple Tree Effect. Picture an apple tree. The apples at the top look perfect — the biggest, the sweetest, the most unblemished. So you keep staring upward, refusing to pick the ones hanging right in front of you. And eventually, you walk away empty-handed.

Here's what this actually looks like. You get a job offer. Good pay, good culture, decent work. But you've heard there might be an interview at a company that sounds even better. So you wait. That interview happens, and then another lead comes up. Months pass. You're still in "waiting mode" — and the original offer? Long gone. This same pattern shows up in choosing partners, making big purchases, mapping out career paths. The core fear isn't making a mistake. It's accepting something good and, in doing so, permanently closing the door on something theoretically better.

And that's actually the key distinction between this and perfectionism, because people mix these up. A perfectionist fears *doing wrong* — they fixate on executing one option flawlessly. The Apple Tree Effect is about fearing *choosing wrong* — you recognize a good option quickly, but you can't commit to it, because somewhere out there, something better might exist. One is paralysis at the execution level. The other is paralysis at the comparison level. They look similar from the outside, but the remedies are totally different.

Now here's the uncomfortable truth about "best choices" in the real world: you can't actually determine the best option without committing to one first. You don't get to audit all the alternatives. Psychologist Barry Schwartz wrote about this in *The Paradox of Choice* — he found that people who always chase the optimal outcome are actually *less satisfied* with their decisions afterward. Why? Because their reference point is never "how good is what I have?" It's always "how good might the thing I didn't pick have been?" You're perpetually competing against a ghost.

So what do you actually do about this? Three things.

First, set criteria, not an optimization target. Before you start evaluating options, decide what conditions the choice needs to meet. When something meets those conditions — take it. Don't keep searching for something that scores higher on a rubric you made up after the fact.

Second, give yourself a search deadline. Define a window. When time's up, pick the best available option. This isn't settling. This is acknowledging that your time and mental energy have real costs, and infinite searching burns both.

Third — and this one's a reframe — stop thinking about commitment as "giving up other possibilities." Start thinking of it as "investing in one direction." Every choice forecloses something. That's not failure, that's just how choices work. What you're really doing when you commit is deciding where to put your future energy. And a good-enough choice you fully invest in will almost always beat a theoretically perfect choice you never made.

So the core takeaways here: The Apple Tree Effect isn't perfectionism — it's a specific fear of missing the optimal, and it needs a different fix. In most real decisions, the "best" option is unknowable until you've already committed, which means chasing it is chasing a mirage. And the antidote is structure: defined criteria, a deadline, and reframing commitment as investment rather than loss.

Let go of the treetop. Pick the apple within reach. It's usually sweet enough.
