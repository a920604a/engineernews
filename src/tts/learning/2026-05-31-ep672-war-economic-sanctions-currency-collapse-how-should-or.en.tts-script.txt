Turkey's lira lost over 40% of its value in a single year. Ukraine froze cross-border transfers within days of the invasion starting. These aren't ancient history — they happened within the last few years. And yet most people's financial planning assumes none of this could ever touch them.

So let's talk about what ordinary people can actually do. Not the ultra-wealthy with offshore accounts and private bankers. Regular people.

Here's the mindset shift that matters: financial risk from geopolitical events isn't a binary switch — it's not "everything is fine" or "total collapse." It's a dial. The real question isn't *whether* your finances are exposed to these kinds of risks. It's *how much* they currently are.

And once you accept that framing, the response becomes a lot less dramatic.

**Start with emergency reserves.** Three to six months of living expenses, sitting in something liquid — a savings account, a money market fund. This sounds boring, but here's why it matters in a crisis: the most financially vulnerable people aren't those with fewer assets. They're the ones whose cash flow disruption immediately forces them into bad decisions. Emergency reserves don't give you comfort. They give you time. Time to think clearly instead of acting under pressure.

**Then think about diversification — but more broadly than stocks versus bonds.** In the context of actual financial resilience, diversification means: do you hold any assets denominated in a foreign currency? USD-denominated ETFs, a foreign currency savings account — these reduce your exposure to any single currency's fate. It also means asking whether everything you own is tied to one market, one country, one system.

Real assets matter here too. Not gold in the bunker-prepper sense — but understanding that real estate and physical commodities behave differently during inflation or currency devaluation than paper assets do.

**The most overlooked piece is single-point dependencies.** If all your money sits in one bank, all your income comes from one employer, and all your investments are in one market — when that one system gets stressed, you have zero buffer. Using two different banks isn't paranoia. Building a second income stream isn't overkill. Knowing what your insurance actually covers isn't pessimism. These are just basic structural resilience.

One more thing worth knowing: capital controls are real and not unprecedented. In severe crises or wartime, governments have restricted how much you can withdraw, whether you can exchange currency, or whether you can move money abroad. This isn't about hiding money — it's about recognizing that liquidity and diversification built during *normal* times are things you can't easily construct once a crisis is already underway.

Now, the thing I want to push back on: hearing all of this and then doing something drastic. Converting everything to gold. Moving all assets overseas immediately. These panic responses almost always do more harm than good.

Building financial resilience is a process, not a single dramatic move. If you don't have adequate emergency reserves yet, start there. Everything else — currency diversification, multiple income streams, geographic spread — can be layered in gradually.

Three things to walk away with. First: financial vulnerability from extreme events is closer to home than most people assume — but the response is methodical, not panic-driven. Second: the real goal of all these preparations is buying yourself *time* — time to assess and think, rather than being forced to react immediately. Third: start where you are. Emergency reserves first, then gradual diversification, then reducing single-point exposures. You don't need to respond perfectly to a crisis. You just need to be able to hold on a little longer than most.
