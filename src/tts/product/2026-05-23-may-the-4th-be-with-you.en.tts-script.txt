"May the Fourth be with you."

If you've never heard that before, you pause for just a second — and then it lands. "May the Force be with you" is the most iconic line in Star Wars, and "fourth" sounds just close enough to "force" that May 4th became a pun-based holiday. Star Wars Day.

But the story behind this holiday is way more interesting than a single dad joke. It's actually a masterclass in how fan culture, brand strategy, and perfect timing can combine into something worth hundreds of millions of dollars a year.

Here's what most people don't know: the earliest recorded use of "May the Fourth be with you" wasn't from a fan at all. It was 1979, the day Margaret Thatcher was elected UK Prime Minister, and her party ran an ad in the London Evening News that read — and I'm not making this up — "May the Fourth Be with You, Maggie. Congratulations." That's it. That's the origin. A political party making a Star Wars pun to congratulate a newly elected prime minister.

It didn't become a real cultural moment until the internet scaled up. Twitter, Facebook — suddenly fans had a shared space to use May 4th as an annual greeting, a celebration point. A de facto tradition built through pure collective participation. No brand owned it. No company organized it. Fans just... did it, year after year.

And then in 2012, Disney acquired Lucasfilm for around four billion dollars. The new management team sat down, looked at what they'd inherited, and realized: fans had already built them a holiday. All they had to do was not ruin it.

That's actually the hard part.

Disney's move was smart precisely because it wasn't aggressive. They didn't try to *create* May the 4th — they formalized and commercialized something that already existed. And crucially, the tone they struck was always "we're celebrating *with* you," never "please attend our official event." That distinction is everything. Fan-built traditions die the moment they start feeling like a corporate activation.

When Disney+ launched in 2019, May the 4th found its real engine. The streaming platform turned the date into one of its most important content anchors — new Star Wars releases, major announcements, something always landing on May 4th. The day stopped being just a celebration of what existed and became an annual "something new is coming" moment that fans actually anticipate.

And then there's the licensing layer. Lego, Adidas, Samsung, food brands — dozens of companies release Star Wars collaborations specifically around this date. That's not accidental. It's a coordinated strategy that turns a cultural moment into an organized commercial ecosystem. A pun became a supply chain event.

So what's the actual lesson here for anyone thinking about products or brands?

First: the best brand holidays are ones users invented. You see this pattern everywhere — community traditions that start grassroots and get amplified later. The brand's job is amplification, not invention. If you're trying to manufacture a holiday from scratch, you're already behind.

Second: participation beats exclusivity every time. Nobody "owns" May 4th. Anyone can post it, celebrate it, make a meme about it. The moment you gate a cultural moment behind a purchase or a membership, you lose the viral spread that makes it actually mean something.

And third — this one's specifically interesting for subscription products: holidays work as rhythm anchors. Disney+ essentially gave subscribers a standing reason to open the app on May 4th every single year. That kind of external cultural anchor creates retention motivation that no push notification can replicate.

May the 4th is, at its core, a pun. A pun that fans kept alive for decades, that a corporation inherited at exactly the right moment, and that got scaled into a global annual event worth hundreds of millions of dollars.

It didn't work because Disney's marketing team is brilliant. It worked because a genuinely passionate fan community spent years pouring their love into this one date — and Disney was disciplined enough to show up in the room without rearranging the furniture.
