And that's exactly what makes it different from going abroad as an expat professional. Expat employment means you take your existing skills and land a real role that uses them. If you stay and build, you gain career continuity, professional depth, a local network that compounds over time. But what you give up is that specific experience that's really only available to you at this particular stage of life. Neither one is better. That's the whole point — every choice has a cost, and being willing to name the cost out loud is what makes it a clear-eyed choice instead of a fantasy.

Okay, so when does a working holiday actually make sense? Three green lights.

First, when you've got a specific learning goal. You're going to work in a kitchen because you want to understand the Australian restaurant scene before you open your own place. Or you want a year immersed in an English-speaking environment to build genuine, real-world business English. The experience has a hook — something that grabs onto your future and pulls it forward.

Second, when there's a natural pause in your career anyway. You just finished a degree. You just left a job. A project just wrapped. These are windows where the cost of stopping is genuinely low, because you'd be between things regardless.

And third — this is the big one — when you already know what you'll do when you come back. The plan doesn't have to be laser-precise. But the direction has to be clear. Because "I'll figure it out when I get back" almost always means the working holiday quietly becomes an expensive way to postpone a decision you were always going to have to make.

Now the flip side. When should you think twice?

When you're just running away. If your current job is painful, a working holiday tends to suspend the problem, not solve it. You come home, and the same situation is right there waiting — now with the added fun of explaining a gap on your resume.

When you can't articulate how the experience will be useful. Look, "I want to see the world" is a completely legitimate reason. But if you can't even tell *yourself* — "I saw the world, and... then what?" — then don't expect the working holiday to do much for your career.

And when the timing is genuinely bad. If you're early in a high-growth company, on a promotion track, in a field where continuity really matters — pausing there costs more than it would at almost any other point in your life.
