Ever notice how some people just... do things? They say they're moving abroad, and then they're gone. They decide to start a company, and they actually start it. And your first reaction is usually one of two things: "Wow, they're so brave," or maybe, a little more skeptically, "They just don't think things through."

But there's a third explanation, and it comes from episode 666 of the podcast 大人的Small Talk. And it flips the whole thing on its head. Those fearless people? They're not fundamentally braver than you. They've just seen enough people.

Let me unpack that, because it's the whole idea.

The hosts, Bryan and Joe, make this argument: the fear we feel when making a big decision doesn't usually come from an accurate read on the risk. It comes from uncertainty. From simply not knowing.

Think about "moving abroad." For a lot of people, that's a terrifying thought. But if you slow down and ask why it's terrifying — it's almost never because you're sitting there thinking, "I know for a fact that life abroad will be miserable." No. It's because you have no idea what life abroad would even be like. It's a blank. And your brain fills that blank with dread.

Now, the people who seem fearless — they've got a different picture in their heads. Their lives are full of reference points. They've got a friend who tried it. A relative who moved overseas. Someone they know who started a business and crushed it, and someone else who started one and it flopped. So for them, "moving abroad" or "starting a company" has quietly shifted category. It's gone from "total unknown" to "a risk with an actual shape." Something you can look at and size up.

And here's a nuance that really matters: seeing enough people does not mean knowing a bunch of successful people. It means seeing enough people who did the thing — and then just... kept living. Including the ones who failed. Who came back. Who adjusted the plan. And ended up basically okay.

That's the piece we skip. We think we need proof of success. What we actually need is proof of survival.

So why does this framework matter so much? Because it changes the question you're asking yourself. Instead of "Am I brave enough?" — which is a miserable, dead-end question, because you're either born bold or you're not — you get to ask, "Are my reference points rich enough yet?" And that second question has a door in it. You can go build reference points. On purpose.

Here's a great example the hosts bring up — the sibling pattern. You've probably noticed it. The older kid in a family tends to be more cautious, more risk-averse. The younger one's often the wild card, the adventurer. Why? Because the younger sibling spent years watching the older one try stuff, mess it up, cry about it, and then be totally fine a week later. That kid got a free education in "failing isn't the end of the world." The oldest child had no one to watch. They were the experiment.

So let's talk about the actual mechanism, because it's simple and kind of beautiful. Fear is at its absolute biggest not when you know the outcome will be bad — it's when you have no clue what the outcome will be. A total blank scares you more than a known disaster. What reference points do is compress that total unknown down into bounded uncertainty. It puts walls around the fear. And once there are walls, once the thing has edges, you can finally do the thing you couldn't do before: actually weigh the tradeoffs and make a real decision. Either way — go or don't go — it becomes an active choice instead of paralysis.

And this is exactly where the "courage" story falls apart. We love to explain daring as a personality trait. He's just a bold guy. She's just naturally cautious. But look at what that framing leaves you with. If it's personality, there's nothing you can do — you just stand there and envy them. Whereas if it's about reference points, the move is obvious: go find people who have actually done the thing.

Now — important caveat. This framework is not saying everyone should go abroad or everyone should start a business. It's saying something gentler and more useful: your "I could never do that" might really just be "I haven't seen enough people do it yet." And that — you can change.

So, practically, what do you actually do?

First: find people who've done the thing, and get the real story out of them. Not the highlight reel. Success stories are almost useless here, because they only show you the polished parts. What you want is the person who did it, it didn't work out, and they're still standing. And you ask them the honest questions. What was the worst case that you were scared of — and did it actually happen? If you could rewind, would you do it again? What surprised you most, the thing you never saw coming? Those conversations build an honest reference point instead of some airbrushed fantasy.

Second: deliberately put yourself around different kinds of lives. Here's the trap most of us are in — our social circles are weirdly homogeneous. Same schools, same career track, same life choices, all clustered together. And in that bubble, anything off the standard path looks insanely risky. Not because it is — but because you have literally never watched what happens to a person who takes it. So widening your circle isn't about networking or collecting contacts for some advantage. It's about actually seeing people who chose differently, and watching how their lives turned out. That's the most natural way there is to get richer reference points.

So let me leave you with the core of it.

One: envying someone's courage is a waste of time, because courage might not even be the real variable. The real variable is how many "people who did the thing" you've actually seen.

Two: fear lives in the unknown, not in the bad outcome. Reference points don't make the risk disappear — they give it a shape, and a shape is something you can decide about.

And three: next time you catch yourself thinking "they're so brave, I could never" — swap the question. Ask instead: how many people do I actually know who've done this, and what happened to them? If the honest answer is "none" or "no idea," then the bottleneck was never your courage. Your reference points just aren't rich enough yet. And that? That you can go fix.
