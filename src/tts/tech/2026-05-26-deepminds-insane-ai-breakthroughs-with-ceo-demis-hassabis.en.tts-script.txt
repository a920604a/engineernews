When Google paid half a billion pounds for DeepMind in 2014, the tech press mostly shrugged. "A game-playing AI company," they said. A curiosity. A bet. Ten years later, DeepMind's research earned a Nobel Prize in Chemistry. That's not luck. That's a strategy.

So what's the strategy? It comes down to one insight from Demis Hassabis, DeepMind's founder — a man who, before building one of the most influential AI labs in the world, spent his career as a video game designer and a neuroscientist. That background isn't trivia. It explains everything about how DeepMind thinks.

Hassabis noticed something about games: they have clear, unambiguous rules for winning and losing. That clarity — that built-in evaluation function — is exactly what you need to train a machine learning system through reinforcement learning. The machine tries things, gets scored, and gets better. No human labels required. Just play, score, iterate.

So DeepMind started with games. AlphaGo, AlphaZero. The point was never to become a world-class chess engine. The point was to prove you could train a general reasoning system by giving it a domain with clear feedback. And once you've proven that, the next question is obvious: what else has clear feedback?

Science does.

Protein folding had been molecular biology's great unsolved problem for fifty years. The challenge: given the sequence of amino acids in a protein, predict what three-dimensional shape it folds into. Shape determines function. Function determines whether a drug works or doesn't. Getting this right is foundational to medicine. And critically — there's a scoring system for how accurate your prediction is. That's your evaluation function.

AlphaFold2 showed up to the CASP14 competition in 2020 and solved it. Not incrementally better than previous methods — it essentially closed the problem. Hassabis and the team behind AlphaFold won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2024. The database they released now contains over 200 million predicted protein structures, covering nearly every known protein sequence on earth. It's freely available to any researcher who wants it.

That's one domain. But DeepMind kept going.

AlphaGeometry, released in early 2024, tackles International Mathematical Olympiad geometry problems — the kind that stump most professional mathematicians. It performs at near-gold-medalist level. And here's the detail that matters: it doesn't just get the right answer. It produces step-by-step proofs that humans can follow and verify. The reasoning is legible.

Then there's AlphaDev. In 2023, DeepMind used reinforcement learning to let an AI autonomously design CPU instruction sequences — actual low-level assembly code — and it discovered a sorting algorithm faster than anything humans had found in 49 years. That algorithm was subsequently adopted into the LLVM C++ standard library. The AI didn't just match human performance. It found something we missed.

And GNoME — Graph Networks for Materials Exploration — predicted 2.2 million potentially stable crystal structures in a single research push. To put that in perspective: humanity discovered around 48,000 stable materials over the past 800 years. GNoME found the equivalent of 45 times that number. About 380,000 of those predictions are considered strong candidates for real-world materials research.

Now, there's a useful contrast here with OpenAI's approach. OpenAI built a general large language model and extended outward from there — better language understanding leads to better reasoning leads to better everything. That path gave us the way humans interact with AI today.

DeepMind's path looks different. For each domain with a clear evaluation function, design a specialized system to crack one specific, scientifically significant problem. AlphaFold isn't GPT. GNoME isn't a chatbot. These are purpose-built machines aimed at specific bottlenecks in human knowledge. The two approaches aren't in competition — they're just different philosophies. But the outcomes are different too. GPT changed how we work. The Alpha series changed the boundaries of what we know.

The underlying template is always the same: find a problem where winning and losing is unambiguous, generate massive training data — often synthetic, often AI-generated — and then let the system self-improve until it surpasses the human frontier. AlphaGo Zero learned chess from scratch with zero human game data. It started knowing only the rules, played itself billions of times, and became the best player on earth. That's the playbook.

Three things to take away from this.

First, the evaluation function is everything. DeepMind doesn't pick problems because they're prestigious or fundable. They pick problems where you can quantifiably measure progress. Without that, reinforcement learning has nowhere to go.

Second, scientific impact and commercial investment aren't opposites. Google's half-billion-pound bet eventually produced a Nobel Prize and a protein database used by researchers worldwide. Choosing hard problems with downstream leverage is both good science and good strategy.

Third — and this one's for engineers — the methodology is borrowable. In whatever domain you work in, there's probably a core bottleneck that, if automated, would unlock a cascade of value downstream. The question DeepMind keeps asking is: can you build an evaluation function for it? If yes, you have a target. And if you have a target, you have a machine learning problem.

That's the playbook. From game boards to Nobel Prizes — same strategy, different boards.
