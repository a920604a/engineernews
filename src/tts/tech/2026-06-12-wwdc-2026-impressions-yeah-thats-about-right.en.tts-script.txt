WWDC 2026 just wrapped, and the headline is this: Apple finally fixed Siri. Like, actually fixed it. Not "added one more feature to the same broken thing" fixed it — rebuilt it from scratch.

Let's talk about what that means.

The new Siri is a completely standalone app. It has access to your emails, your messages, your photos, your calendar, your files — it's a genuine personal context engine now, not the voice-activated Google search shortcut it's been for the past decade. You ask it something, and it actually knows things about *you* specifically. That's the big shift.

Here's the technical detail that raised a lot of eyebrows: the brain powering this new Siri is Google Gemini. Apple is routing queries through their Private Cloud Compute architecture, and the claim is that your personal data doesn't get used to train Gemini — inference runs in an isolated environment. Whether you trust that or not is a whole separate conversation. But architecturally, it's a significant admission from Apple that they couldn't build the LLM they needed in-house, at least not in time.

Now, here's the catch — and there are a few. First, it's waitlisted. Not everyone gets it day one. Second, if you're in the EU, you don't get it at all on launch day, thanks to Digital Markets Act complications. And third — and this is the one developers should actually care about — there's no third-party API. Apple rebuilt Siri from the ground up and then... kept the door shut for everyone else. Apps can't deeply integrate with this new AI Siri yet. That story is probably coming at WWDC 2027.

Moving on to macOS. The new version is called Golden Gate, and it's making one thing very clear: the Intel Mac era is over. Golden Gate is the last version of macOS that will run on Intel hardware. If you bought a Mac with Apple Silicon — anything M1 or later from 2020 onward — you're fine. If you're still on an Intel MacBook Pro, you're not being forced off a cliff today, but you're definitely standing at the edge.

For developers specifically, this matters beyond just your personal machine. If your team is running CI pipelines or build servers on Intel Mac hardware, the clock is ticking. You have until the *next* major macOS release before you're frozen out of new OS features.

Golden Gate also brings Visual Intelligence into Spotlight, which means you can query using content that's actually on your screen. The toolbar and sidebar get unified across apps, AirDrop gets faster, network file browsing speeds up, and Messages sync improves. Solid, useful updates.

Then there's iOS 27, which honestly has a story that's almost funny. You might remember iOS 26 introduced "Liquid Glass" — this translucent, fluid design language that looked beautiful in marketing materials and was kind of a readability nightmare in real life. The complaints were loud enough that iOS 27 ships with an opacity slider. Users can now dial the transparency down to something comfortable, all the way to fully opaque if they want. Apple didn't walk it back; they just gave you a knob. Pragmatic.

iOS 27 also adds custom EQ settings for AirPods per device, more granular parental controls that go app by app and website by website, and it runs on iPhone 11 and later.

On the Apple Intelligence side — Image Playground can now generate photorealistic images, not just the stylized animations and illustrations it was limited to before. That's a meaningful upgrade. And in the Photos app, there are two new tools worth knowing: Extend, which stretches image edges outward — think Adobe's Generative Fill — and Reframe, which lets you adjust composition and fill empty space. Clean Up also got better object detection. The Home app gets natural language search through security camera footage, which is genuinely useful if you've ever tried to find "that one moment from yesterday afternoon" in hours of camera recordings.

So stepping back — what's the actual takeaway from WWDC 2026?

Three things. First, Siri is real now, finally, but it's coming out slowly and with significant geographic and access restrictions. Manage your expectations for the next six months. Second, macOS dropping Intel support is the most operationally consequential change for anyone running development infrastructure — if you haven't made that hardware transition yet, this is the moment to prioritize it. And third, Apple is playing catch-up in AI, and they know it. Partnering with Google for Siri's backend is not the move of a company that thinks it's winning that race yet.

Developer beta is out now. Public beta hits in July. General availability comes in September alongside the iPhone 18 Pro.
