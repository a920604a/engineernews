That backflip Boston Dynamics showed the world in 2017? It broke the internet. What most people didn't know is that Atlas was running on hydraulic actuators — insanely powerful, yes, but also power-hungry, maintenance-intensive, and basically impossible to manufacture at scale. It was a demonstration, not a product.

Seven years later, the question isn't "can a robot move?" anymore. The question is "can we build ten thousand of them, have them work reliably for ten thousand hours, and not go bankrupt doing it?" That's a completely different problem. And the answer lives not in the AI — it lives in the hardware.

Let's talk about what's actually hard here.

Every humanoid robot is balancing three things that don't want to cooperate: performance, manufacturability, and cost. High performance points you toward hydraulics or high-torque motors. Manufacturability points you toward simpler, standardized electric systems. And cost punishes you every time you reach for a custom part instead of something off the shelf. The current industry consensus — electric motors paired with harmonic drives — is basically an agreement to sacrifice some raw power in exchange for something you can actually build in a factory.

Now, harmonic drives. These are the unsung heroes and the biggest headache at the same time. A harmonic drive is a compact gearbox that can take a fast, low-torque motor and turn it into the slow, high-torque movement a robot joint actually needs — ratios of a hundred to one are common. They fit in tight spaces, they're precise, and they're everywhere in humanoid robots. A single robot might have twenty to forty joints, each needing one. The problem? The global supply is almost entirely in the hands of two Japanese companies — Harmonic Drive and Nabtesco — plus a small number of Chinese manufacturers. One harmonic drive can cost anywhere from hundreds to thousands of dollars. Multiply that across forty joints and you're looking at a serious chunk of your bill of materials before you've even added a camera.

In 2024, when Figure, Unitree, Zhiyuan, and Physical Intelligence all started ramping toward mass production at the same time, harmonic drive lead times started stretching. This is the "everyone fishing in the same pond" problem, and it's very real.

The alternative approach — pioneered by MIT's Cheetah project — is something called quasi-direct drive. You use much lower gear ratios, which means the robot can actually feel external forces pushing back on its joints. It's more natural, more compliant, better for dynamic movement. The tradeoff is you give up torque density. Different philosophy, different tradeoffs.

Then there's sensing. Think of sensing as two categories: the robot knowing what its own body is doing, and the robot knowing what the world around it is doing.

The first category — joint position, velocity, torque — is the foundation of stable walking. You need high-precision encoders in every joint, and they have to stay reliable under constant vibration and mechanical stress. Solved? Mostly. Hard? Still yes.

The second category is where things get genuinely humbling. Dexterous hand tactile sensing. A human hand has over seventeen thousand tactile receptors. Current robot finger sensors are either too low-resolution to be useful, too fragile to survive real work, or too expensive to put in a product. The data flows like this: cameras, depth sensors, and inertial measurement units feed into a perception system alongside joint torque data, and all of that gets processed into control decisions that drive the actuators. The tactile sensors in the hand are supposed to be part of that loop — but right now, they're the weakest link in the chain. "Catching a falling leaf" requires sensing an almost negligible force, precisely and instantly. That is technically harder than a backflip. Not even close.

On the structural side, it's a familiar engineering negotiation. Carbon fiber is light and stiff but expensive and difficult to manufacture at scale. Aluminum is heavier but cheap and well-understood. Titanium offers a great strength-to-weight ratio but is costly enough that you use it sparingly, only at the highest-stress joints. Engineering plastics handle shells and non-load-bearing parts where you just need something light and easy to mold. No single material wins everywhere — the answer is always a mix, which makes assembly more complex.

And assembly complexity is its own bottleneck. Humanoid robots are significantly harder to assemble than cars. Every joint needs precise clearance tolerances. Running cables through a structure with dozens of degrees of freedom is genuinely painful to design for. Seals and waterproofing have to survive constant flexing and bending without failing. Boston Dynamics didn't get good at this overnight — it took more than a decade to build those manufacturing capabilities.

Finally, batteries. Today's humanoid robots typically run one to three hours on a charge. That's a real limitation for industrial use cases. The energy density of lithium batteries sets a hard ceiling on how much power you can store within a given weight budget. If solid-state batteries hit commercial viability somewhere in the 2027 to 2030 window — and that's a genuine possibility — the impact on robotics could be as transformative as any AI breakthrough.

So here's what to take away from all of this.

First: the bottleneck in humanoid robotics right now is not AI — it's hardware. Specifically, high-precision harmonic drives in short supply, tactile sensing that isn't mature enough for real dexterity, and battery energy density that limits working hours.

Second: the supply chain is fragile and concentrated. When multiple companies race toward mass production simultaneously, they're all pulling from the same small pool of specialized suppliers. That creates risk that has nothing to do with engineering talent.

Third: there's no single winning design yet. Figure, Tesla's Optimus, and Unitree's H1 have made different bets across actuators, sensing, and materials. The industry is still in the phase where multiple approaches coexist — and that means the design choices being made right now will have long tails.

The transition from "can it work" to "can we ship ten thousand of them" is where the real engineering lives. And it's happening right now.
