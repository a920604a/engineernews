SpaceX isn't just a rocket company anymore. People have said that for years — but until now, it was more of a vibe than a fact. Then the S-1 filing landed, and suddenly there were numbers on the page. Real ones. And those numbers tell a story that's very different from the one you'd expect from a company famous for launching rockets.

So here's the headline. SpaceX plans to go public on the Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX, at one hundred thirty-five dollars a share, at a valuation of one-point-seven-five trillion dollars. It's aiming to raise seventy-five billion. That would make it the largest IPO in stock market history. Full stop.

But the number that actually matters — the one that reframes how you should think about this whole company — is this: fifty-eight percent of SpaceX's revenue comes from Starlink. The satellite internet service. And here's the kicker — Starlink is the only part of the business that actually makes money. One-point-one-nine billion dollars in net profit in 2024. The rocket launch business? On paper, it loses money. It's being kept alive by Starlink's cash flow.

Let me sit with that for a second, because it's genuinely counterintuitive. The rockets are the thing everyone watches. The booster landing itself upright on a drone ship in the middle of the ocean — that's the viral moment. But financially, the rockets aren't the engine. The internet subscription you pay every month is.

Let's break the company into its three pieces, because the S-1 lays it out cleanly. First, Launch — that's Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy, and the big new one, Starship. Second, Starlink — consumer and enterprise broadband beamed down from low Earth orbit. And third, Starshield — the government and military version, secure communications for the U.S. government.

Now, why is Starlink the real commercial engine? The numbers again. Roughly ten-point-three million subscribers as of late 2025. Fifty-eight percent of total revenue. And that one-point-one-nine billion in profit I mentioned. Launch is a technical marvel — Falcon 9's first stage is very nearly a hundred percent reusable — but the cost structure just doesn't let you make money on launches alone. And Starship costs even more to develop. So the whole operation, this trillion-dollar machine, runs on subscription fees landing in the bank from ten-million-plus households and businesses, every single month.

Okay, but a one-point-seven-five trillion dollar valuation — how do you even justify that? Let's walk the logic. If you value Starlink on its own, like a subscription software business: ten-point-three million subscribers, each paying somewhere around a hundred to a hundred-twenty dollars a month. Annualize that and you get recurring revenue of maybe twelve to fifteen billion dollars. Apply the kind of multiple a high-margin services business commands — call it ten to twelve times revenue — and Starlink by itself is worth something like a hundred-twenty to a hundred-eighty billion.

Then you stack the rest on top. Falcon 9 dominates global launch — more than sixty percent of all orbital launches on Earth. Starship opens up a long-term market that's almost science fiction: deep space, a lunar base, Mars. And Starshield has that sticky government-contract moat. Put it all together, and one-point-seven-five trillion is aggressive — but it's not pulled out of thin air.

Now the part every honest investor has to reckon with: the risks. And the S-1 is refreshingly blunt about them. Risk number one — Elon Musk. The filing itself says, in writing, that Musk's personal brand and his decision-making are central to the company. And in the same breath it admits he's simultaneously running Tesla, xAI, and X. That's a lot of companies for one brain. Risk number two — competition. Amazon's Project Kuiper is coming, the EU has a constellation called IRIS-squared, and China has multiple state-backed satellite programs in the pipeline. Risk number three — Starship is still Starship. It's the next-generation core of the whole plan, but it's still in testing, and the commercial timeline is genuinely uncertain.

Let me put on the engineering hat for a moment, because this is where it gets interesting. The reason any of this works comes down to reusable rockets changing the cost math. Traditional rockets were throwaway — you flew once and dropped the hardware in the ocean. Falcon 9's booster comes back and flies again — the current record is a single booster flying more than twenty times. That drops the cost of putting a kilogram into orbit from the old world of five to ten thousand dollars, down to around twenty-seven hundred. And that cost collapse is the entire reason it made economic sense to launch thousands of Starlink satellites in the first place. Starship pushes the target even further — fully reusable, aiming for under a hundred dollars per kilogram. That's an order of magnitude cheaper than Falcon 9. If they actually hit it, the economics of space change completely.

And here's the thing about Starlink's real moat — it isn't just the roughly seven thousand satellites already up there. It's vertical integration. SpaceX builds its own satellites, cheaply. It launches them on its own rockets, cheaply. And it operates the whole constellation itself, so it can optimize end to end. Compare that to the competition. Amazon's Kuiper has to pay someone else to launch its satellites. OneWeb had to be rebuilt after it was acquired. Nobody else has all three pieces under one roof, and you can't copy that overnight.

So what should you actually watch from here? Three things. One — does the price hold? Can public markets really swallow one-point-seven-five trillion in 2026? For context, that's below Nvidia and Apple, which sit above three trillion. So it fits inside the mega-cap tech range. But SpaceX's profitability doesn't yet match those companies, and that gap is the whole debate. Two — Starship commercialization. After the IPO, the next big milestone is Starship flying real, full-scale commercial missions. Three — Musk's calendar. Investors are being asked to accept, out loud, that the man at the center is also running three other major companies at the same time.

So let me leave you with the three things that really matter here. First: SpaceX is, financially, an internet company wearing a rocket company's clothes — Starlink is fifty-eight percent of revenue and the only profitable division, and it's paying for everything else. Second: the whole edifice rests on reusable rockets driving the cost of reaching orbit down by an order of magnitude, which is what made mass satellite deployment viable in the first place. And third: the biggest question mark isn't the technology — it's whether the market pays a trillion-and-three-quarters for profits that haven't fully arrived yet, with a founder whose attention is split four ways. That's the bet. Now we find out if the market takes it.
