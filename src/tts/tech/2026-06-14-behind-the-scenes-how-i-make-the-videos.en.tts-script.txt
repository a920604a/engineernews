That ten-minute tech video you just watched? It took somewhere between eleven and twenty-two hours to make. Let's break down exactly where that time goes — because the production workflow for a tech YouTube channel has a lot more in common with software engineering than most people realize.

Topic selection is basically backlog management. The filtering question most tech creators use is: "Can I explain this clearly in under ten minutes to an engineer who actually cares?" Sources are what you'd expect — ArXiv preprints, AI lab blogs from places like Anthropic or DeepMind, GitHub Trending, Hacker News. But also: personal frustrations, and the comment sections of your own previous videos.

Here's the strategic insight that separates channels that compound from channels that plateau: prioritize evergreen over news. A video explaining how Kafka's architecture works keeps pulling in views for three years. A video about a product launch fades in two weeks. Unless it's historically significant, the news cycle is a treadmill.

Research is the longest phase — four to eight hours for a single ten-minute video. The target depth isn't "can I restate the documentation." It's "can I answer comment questions." Those are very different bars.

The current AI-assisted workflow: use Claude or ChatGPT for a first-pass synthesis. Structure a paper's main claims into a list, flag which points feel uncertain, then verify those specific points against primary sources. That last step is not optional. AI synthesis sometimes fills gaps with confident-sounding "common knowledge" that isn't actually in the source. Trust but verify, every time.

Scripting a ten-minute video means writing roughly fifteen hundred to eighteen hundred words. And here's the thing most people get wrong: spoken syntax and reading syntax are not the same. Readers can scroll back. Listeners cannot. A good script completes each concept fully before moving on, rather than assuming the audience remembers what was said two minutes ago.

The structure that works: hook in the first fifteen seconds — that's when viewers decide whether to stay. Then one clean sentence defining the topic. Then why you should care. Then three to five core concepts. Then the "so what" — the actual takeaway. AI is useful for drafting speed in the middle sections, but the hook and the conclusion need to be written by hand. That's where personality shows up.

Recording. The most commonly asked question is "what camera do you use?" And the answer is almost always: camera is not the bottleneck. A Sony ZV-E10 with a good key light and a decent condenser mic will outperform a much more expensive camera in a dark, echoey room. Lighting and audio quality are what viewers perceive as "production quality," even if they can't articulate why.

Most tech creators use a combination of talking head — direct to camera — and screen recordings for code walkthroughs and demos. One practical tip that consistently gets mentioned: stand while recording. Voice quality is noticeably better standing than sitting for an hour. A motorized sit-stand desk pays for itself in reduced pickup time.

A ten-minute video generates thirty to fifty minutes of raw footage, including retakes. You don't need to nail the full script in one go — most people memorize and record section by section.

Editing. The dominant tools are DaVinci Resolve — the free version handles everything most creators need — followed by Final Cut Pro for Mac users, and then Premiere. Premiere's subscription cost has pushed a lot of people toward DaVinci.

The editing sequence goes: rough cut to remove obvious mistakes and dead air, then audio processing for noise reduction, then subtitles, then B-roll insertion, color grading, and finally intro and outro.

AI has most changed two specific steps. Subtitles used to take hours of manual transcription — Whisper-based tools now generate accurate subtitles in minutes, with only a handful of errors to correct by hand. Audio noise reduction is similarly automated now. The actual editorial judgment in the cut — what stays, what goes, what order things run in — that's still manual.

Thumbnails. This is the step people underestimate until they understand the algorithm. Thumbnail determines click-through rate. Click-through rate determines algorithmic reach. A technically excellent video with a bad thumbnail gets buried. The rules are simple but strict: large text, high contrast, include a human face — faces attract attention faster than icons. Keep the subject text to five words or fewer, because at mobile scale on a small phone screen, anything longer is just a blur. Test it at quarter size. If you can't identify it, redo it.

Title and description drive search discoverability. The title needs to simultaneously contain the right keywords and make someone want to click — without being clickbait. The first two lines of the description, visible before the "show more" collapse, should contain the topic keywords. Chapter timestamps measurably improve average watch duration. These are small details with real impact.

On timing: for Taiwan-based channels, Wednesday or Thursday morning tends to work well — it covers morning commute time locally, and catches early evening the previous day on the US West Coast.

So what does the time budget actually look like? Topic selection and research takes the biggest chunk: four to eight hours. Scripting is two to four. Recording itself is only one to two — it's a small fraction of the total. Editing runs three to six hours. Thumbnails and metadata are another one to two. Total: eleven to twenty-two hours per video. AI tools have compressed roughly twenty to thirty percent of that, mostly on the research and subtitle sides.

Three things to take away from this. First, research depth is the actual moat — most people underinvest here, and it shows in comment sections. Second, audio and lighting matter far more than camera hardware. Third, AI has changed the workflow at specific leverage points — synthesis and subtitles — but human judgment in topic selection, scripting voice, and editorial decisions hasn't left the loop. It's just applied at different stages.

If you make tech content or are thinking about it, the workflow is learnable. The time investment is real. And the compounding is also real — evergreen content doesn't stop working.
