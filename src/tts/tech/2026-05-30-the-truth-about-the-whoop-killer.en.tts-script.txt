Every few months, some new wearable gets crowned a "Whoop killer" in the tech press. And every few months, Whoop is still there — running subscriptions, keeping loyal users, holding its ground with elite athletes and serious fitness people. So the real question isn't which device looks most like Whoop. It's: where exactly is Whoop's moat, and has anyone actually crossed it?

Let's start with what Whoop actually is, for anyone not already deep in the fitness tracking world. It's a screenless wearable — no display, no notifications, just sensors. You pay a monthly subscription and the hardware comes free. Every morning you get three numbers: your Recovery score, your Strain score from the previous day, and a breakdown of your sleep. Recovery is the big one — it's calculated from your heart rate variability, resting heart rate, and sleep quality, and it tells you whether your body is ready to push hard or whether you should dial it back. That's the whole product. It's not trying to be your smartwatch. It's trying to be your body's daily status report.

The core user is someone who trains seriously — triathletes, CrossFit regulars, marathon runners. People who want to make training decisions based on actual physiological data, not just how they feel walking out of bed.

Now, 2026 has given us three real challengers.

First is Google Fitbit Air, which landed in May at ninety-nine dollars with no subscription required. Google formally rebranded Fitbit as Google Health and made the subscription model disappear entirely. The headline feature is the Gemini Health Coach — instead of just showing you your numbers, it has a conversation with you about them. Something like: "Your HRV has been dropping for three weeks in a row, which lines up with your shorter sleep windows — want to think about adjusting today's intensity?" That's genuinely different from what any tracker has done before. The advantages are obvious: no ongoing cost, Google ecosystem integration, and Gemini's AI capabilities. The question marks are equally obvious: Fitbit has had quality baggage from previous generations, and Google has a complicated history of actually sticking with health products long-term.

Second is the Garmin Cirqa, which is rumored for 2026 and appears to be a direct shot across Whoop's bow. Screenless design, recovery-first focus, HRV monitoring, skin temperature, blood oxygen — the hardware spec sheet reads like Garmin studied Whoop and said "we can do this." Garmin's brand is extremely strong with serious runners and cyclists, and the subscription cost is expected to come in lower than Whoop's. The weakness? Garmin's app has always been powerful but not exactly a joy to use. And realistically, this device is probably most appealing to people already in the Garmin ecosystem.

Third is Apple Watch — specifically after watchOS 11, which introduced Training Load tracking and brought Apple Watch into Whoop's core territory. The Ultra 2 pushed battery life to sixty hours, which makes all-day wear actually practical rather than aspirational. Apple's advantages here are almost unfair: the largest wearable user base on the planet, deep integration with Apple Health, and the iOS ecosystem. The limitation is that recovery tracking is just one item on a very long feature list for Apple Watch. Building a focused "recovery culture" around a device that also does your messages, your payments, and your navigation is a genuinely different challenge.

Here's the thing though — at the hardware level, Whoop's moat is actually pretty shallow. HRV monitoring, optical heart rate, sleep tracking — these are all mature technologies. Fitbit, Garmin, and Apple Watch have all been at hardware parity for a while. The real moat is somewhere else entirely.

It's behavioral. Whoop users are conditioned. Checking your recovery score is the first thing you do every morning, and it changes what you do that day. Once that habit forms — once your whole training framework is built around those three numbers — the cost of switching isn't just learning a new app. It's recalibrating how you understand your own body. That's a high psychological barrier.

On top of that, there's identity. In marathon circles and triathlon communities, "are you on Whoop?" is a form of recognition. That culture doesn't transfer when you copy the feature set. And then there's data history — if you have a year or more of biometric baselines on Whoop, starting over on a new platform means months of less accurate scores while it relearns you.

So where does this land?

Three things to take away. One: the subscription-free model that Fitbit Air is pioneering is the most structurally threatening move anyone has made against Whoop — not because it's better hardware, but because it removes the biggest adoption barrier for everyone who's been curious but hesitant. Two: Garmin is the most credible hardware challenger in the serious athlete market specifically, but it's fighting for a slice of an existing community rather than expanding it. Three: Apple is the wildcard that Whoop should actually lose sleep over. Not because Apple Watch beats Whoop today, but because if Apple successfully brings recovery culture into the mainstream — into the hundreds of millions of people already wearing Apple Watches — Whoop's addressable market shrinks from "serious fitness community" to a niche that gets much harder to defend.

The Whoop killer might not arrive in 2026. But the pressure is real, and it's coming from three very different directions at once.
