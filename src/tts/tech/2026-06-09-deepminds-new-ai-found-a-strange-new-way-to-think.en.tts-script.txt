In July 2024, DeepMind dropped something that went way beyond the usual AI benchmark news. Their system, AlphaProof, sat down with the International Mathematical Olympiad — one of the hardest math competitions in the world — and solved four out of six problems. One of those problems was the hardest in the entire set, and only five human competitors in the world managed to crack it. That's not an incremental improvement. That's a different kind of machine entirely.

So what makes AlphaProof different? To understand that, you have to start with the core weakness of every large language model when it comes to math. These models generate text that *looks* like reasoning. They produce plausible steps, confident conclusions — and absolutely no mechanism to catch themselves when they're wrong mid-proof. There's no internal auditor. It's just pattern matching all the way down, dressed up in mathematical notation.

AlphaProof's designers looked at that problem and decided the answer wasn't to make the language model smarter at generating proofs. The answer was to force every single step to be verified by a completely separate system — one that doesn't do fuzzy pattern matching, one that simply tells you: this step holds, or it doesn't. That system is called Lean, a formal proof language where mathematical logic is machine-checkable. If your proof step doesn't follow from what came before it, Lean rejects it. No ambiguity.

Here's where it gets architecturally interesting. AlphaProof is what people call neurosymbolic — it marries two very different kinds of intelligence. On one side, you have a Gemini-based language model that takes a natural language math problem, translates it into Lean's formal notation, and generates candidate proof steps. It's bringing the statistical intuition, the pattern recognition, the "this kind of structure usually suggests this kind of move."

On the other side, you have a reinforcement learning engine descended from AlphaZero — the same system that taught itself chess, Go, and shogi from scratch by playing millions of games against itself. Here, instead of board positions, it's navigating the space of possible proof paths. Every time Lean verifies a step, that's a signal. Every time a full proof completes, the language model gets stronger on that class of problem. Every time something fails — and most things fail — the system learns from that too.

The critical word here is *verifiability*. When AlphaProof hands you a proof, it's not "probably correct." It's a complete, step-by-step argument that you can recheck yourself in Lean. This is fundamentally different from asking an LLM a math question and getting a confident-sounding answer. The output is either a proof or it isn't. There's no confidence score hiding an error on line seven.

Back to that IMO performance. Under standard Olympic scoring — seven points per problem — solving four problems gives you twenty-eight points. That lands at silver-medal level. Now, AlphaProof didn't finish in the four-and-a-half-hour window human competitors get — speed wasn't the design goal here, correctness was. But what it did produce was four legitimate, fully verified mathematical proofs at competition level.

Then came AlphaProof Nexus, the follow-up. And this is where things shift from "impressive benchmark" to "actual contribution to mathematics." Nexus worked on open problems — things mathematicians haven't solved yet. It proved nine of three hundred fifty-three open Erdős conjectures. It proved forty-four of four hundred ninety-two conjectures from the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences. These aren't test problems with known answers. These are things that have been sitting open in the mathematical literature.

To put the architectural difference in plain terms: compare AlphaProof to a regular language model like o1 or o3. Those systems have no verification layer — whatever they generate, right or wrong, is the output. Compare it to something like Mathematica, which can verify arithmetic and algebraic computation, but only within a narrow computational domain. AlphaProof sits in a different category: formal verification across the full scope of mathematical reasoning, backed by reinforcement learning that actually improves from every failed proof attempt.

So here's what to take away from all of this.

First, the architecture matters more than the benchmark. The real story isn't "AI solved Olympic math." It's that coupling neural intuition with formal verification gives AI genuine error-correction in structured reasoning — something pure language models fundamentally lack.

Second, this extends beyond mathematics. Formal languages don't just describe proofs. They describe software specifications, cryptographic protocols, security properties. The same framework that caught errors in Olympic-level proofs could, in principle, catch errors in system designs where a bug doesn't mean a wrong answer — it means a vulnerability.

And third, AlphaProof Nexus solving open conjectures signals something worth watching closely. When a system transitions from solving known problems to contributing new results, the question stops being "how good is the AI" and starts being "what does this change about how mathematics gets done."
