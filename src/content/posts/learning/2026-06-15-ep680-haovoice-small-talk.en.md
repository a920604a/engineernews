You have books on the shelf you bought months ago and never finished. That bothers you a little.

This has a name: **reading guilt**. You were excited in the bookstore, paid, then life continued, and now that book sits there quietly reminding you of an obligation you haven't met.

Hao Xu-Lie—Taiwanese financial author and host of the *Haovoice* podcast—gives a liberating answer to this feeling in EP680: not finishing is normal, and it's fine.

## Reading's Goal Is Not Completion

Hao's core argument: **the purpose of reading is finding a connection between the book and your own life—not turning all the pages.**

That "connection" can take many forms:
- A concept that suddenly clarifies something you already sensed ("so that's why I've always thought this way")
- A challenge that makes you uncomfortable ("this book's premise completely contradicts my assumptions")
- A method you can apply immediately
- A story that reminds you of your own experience

Once any of these happens, the book has done its job.

## Why Reading Guilt Exists

Reading guilt's source is probably the habit we brought out of school: books are read front to back, you're supposed to remember the content (because there's a test). This framework made sense in school but becomes a liability outside it.

School books are designed to move everyone through the same knowledge at the same pace—"finishing" is a reasonable standard there. But the books you choose in a bookstore were never designed for everyone. They're what you, at a specific point in your life, felt you needed.

What you needed might be on page 30, or page 120, or scattered across several chapters. Page 180 may have no connection to your life right now. Forcing yourself through it burns attention without building understanding.

## Connection Is Harder Than Completion—and More Valuable

"Just find the connection" sounds easy. But the "connection" Hao means isn't shallow recognition ("oh, I know about this")—it's closer to "this relates to something real in my situation and I need to stop and think about it."

When this kind of connection happens, you usually stop. Close the book, or put down your phone. Something turns over in your mind. You might start revisiting a decision you made, or find a new angle on a problem you've been sitting with.

**That's harder to achieve than reading 300 pages with nothing sticking.**

Density of reading isn't about word count—it's about connection density. Some books you finish in a weekend, but a year later you remember nothing. Some books you read three chapters of, but one idea from those three chapters shapes your thinking for years.

## For Engineers Reading Technical Books

This framework applies directly to technical reading. Technical books are usually dense and broad, but you pick them up because of a specific problem: you're learning Kubernetes, studying system design, trying to understand a design pattern.

"Read front to back" is usually the wrong strategy. More effective:
- Scan the table of contents first; find chapters you currently have a connection to
- Read those chapters; when questions arise, navigate to related sections
- Keep the book accessible and return to it when you reach the next point in your life that needs it

Treat a book as a "knowledge repository" rather than a "task to complete," and your relationship to it changes. You don't owe it anything; it's there waiting for you to need it.

## On Buying Books

Hao's other interesting point: buying a book—even one you never finish—reflects your state and curiosity at that moment, which is itself a meaningful signal.

"Why did I think this book mattered to me then?" is a more useful question than "Why didn't I finish it?"

The first helps you understand yourself. The second just produces guilt.
