Time granularity is the smallest unit you use to schedule your time. Most people live their lives in units of hours, excellent managers operate in units of 15 minutes, and the top performers, supposedly, can be precise down to the second. This isn't bragging — it's a different level of perception of the cost of time.

## What Is Time Granularity

Time granularity refers to the smallest unit of time you use when arranging your schedule and making commitments.

For someone who operates in units of "days," saying "see you tomorrow" feels natural. For someone who operates in units of "15 minutes," "tomorrow" isn't even a valid point in time at all — which 15 minutes tomorrow?

There's an example in *Underlying Logic*: a person whose time granularity is hours invites another person to "swing by Tianjin while you're at it" — to him it's just one extra hour, but for someone who plans their time in 15-minute blocks, this might mean an entire afternoon's schedule is destroyed.

**The gap in granularity isn't an efficiency problem — it's a problem of perceiving the cost of other people's time.**

## Three Layers of Time Management

Time granularity isn't a single dimension; it's three layers running simultaneously:

**By the year: goals and direction**
Set annual goals and action plans, and decide which quadrant you spend your time in. Without this layer, all your fine-grained scheduling is just running faster in the wrong direction.

**By the day: allocating discretionary time**
You have roughly 8 hours of freely discretionary time in a day. How you allocate those 8 hours determines whether you're executing on your goals or just dealing with miscellaneous busywork.

**By the hour (or 15 minutes): the battle for focus**
Maintain 100% focus during each time slot. Once you switch to scheduling in 15-minute units, you'll start to realize that "just chatting for a few minutes" doesn't really exist — chatting for 5 minutes is equivalent to eating up the next time block.

## Energy Management > Time Management

Time management has a fundamental blind spot: people aren't machines, and the quality of output varies enormously across different times of day.

Career coach Elvis Lin's view is: **allocating tasks according to the ebb and flow of your energy matters more than fine-grained scheduling.**

Use the two hours in the early morning, when your energy is at its peak, to tackle your hardest deep work. Not for checking email, not for routine meetings. Put the hardest things in your most energized window, and put the things that only require mechanical execution in the windows where your energy is declining.

This connects directly to the concept of Flow — only when the difficulty of the challenge matches your skill level do you easily enter a state of complete immersion where you lose yourself in the work. And the prerequisite for entering flow is having a block of time long enough to go uninterrupted. A fragmented schedule kills flow.

## In Practice: A Few Things You Can Use Right Away

**Switch your calendar to 15-minute slots**
In Google Calendar or Outlook, change your time slots from 1 hour to 15 minutes. This action alone will make you start seeing wasted time you couldn't see before.

**Knock out small tasks instantly**
Anything you can resolve in under 2 minutes, do it now. Don't let it occupy your cognitive bandwidth later. These "small wins" accumulate a sense of progress and momentum, giving you more headroom to handle the harder things later — the point isn't the 2 minutes themselves, but that they no longer occupy your attention in the background.

**On time = late**
Arriving early is being on time. Showing up right at the wire of the scheduled time, to someone who schedules in 15-minute units, means you've wrecked their next time block.

**It only gets easier once it becomes a habit**
The early phase of refining your time granularity is painful, because you have to deliberately think about how to arrange every time slot. But once the habit is formed, it becomes autopilot. It's like learning to ride a bike — at first you have to consciously control your balance, but once you're fluent your brain can do other things.

## Overall

Time granularity is a cognitive problem, not just a tooling problem. Tools (calendars, reminders) only make your intentions visible; the real change is that your underlying perception of the cost of time has changed.

Upgrading from units of hours to units of 15 minutes isn't just about cutting the boxes smaller — it's about starting to genuinely treat time as a non-renewable resource. Once this perception is established, your tolerance for phrases like "while you're at it," "let's just chat for a bit," and "give me a sec" will drop dramatically — this isn't becoming difficult to deal with, it's starting to be honest about your own time and others'.
